A polyomino is rectifiable if copies of it, rotated and reflected as needed, tile some rectangle without overlap. Its order is the fewest copies that tile a rectangle. The concept goes back to Klarner (); see also Golomb ().

Small orders

  • Order 1: the polyomino is itself a rectangle. The catalogue fills these in automatically.
  • Order 2: two copies form a rectangle.
  • Order 3 does not occur: no polyomino has order 3 (Stewart and Wormstein, ).
  • Large orders exist. Dahlke showed that the Y-hexomino has order 92 () and that the heptomino has order 76 ().

Odd order

The odd order is the fewest copies, odd in number, that tile a rectangle. For many pieces every rectangle tiling uses an even number of copies, and then the odd order is recorded as "none". When the order itself is odd, the two numbers agree.

Smallest rectangles and squares

The smallest rectangles are the tileable rectangles of least area. Their area is the order times the number of cells, and the data checker enforces this. There may be several of them, of different shapes. The smallest square is the side of the smallest tileable square, or "none" if no square can be tiled.

Bounds

While a value is open, the catalogue records the bounds known: for example, a lower bound on the order from an exhaustive search, or an upper bound from a known tiling.
